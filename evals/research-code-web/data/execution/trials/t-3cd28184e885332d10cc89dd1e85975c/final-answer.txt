## Answer

**Is PostgreSQL 16 supported under upstream community policy at the 2026‑10‑07 cutoff?**
Yes. The PostgreSQL Global Development Group's own Versioning Policy table lists major version **16** with "Supported: **Yes**," current minor **16.15**, first release **September 14, 2023**, and final release **November 9, 2028** ([postgresql.org/support/versioning](https://www.postgresql.org/support/versioning/)). Because the cutoff (2026‑10‑07) falls well before that final date, 16 is a supported major version at the cutoff. This is the upstream community's own designation, not a vendor's extended-support timeline.

**Scheduled final-release date:** **November 9, 2028** — the date the community table lists under "Final Release" for version 16.

**How long major versions are supported, and when the clock begins:**
The policy states, "The PostgreSQL Global Development Group supports a major version for **5 years after its initial release**. After this, a final minor version will be released and the software will then be unsupported (end-of-life)" ([versioning page](https://www.postgresql.org/support/versioning/)). So the support window is **five years**, and the clock begins at the **major version's initial (first) release**, not at any later minor release. For 16 that anchor date is **September 14, 2023**; five years later is September 2023+5 ≈ 2028, and the community schedules the concluding minor release shortly after that anniversary — here November 9, 2028. (The "final release" is that last minor release containing outstanding fixes; afterward 16 is end-of-life.)

**Does installing a newer 16.x minor restart the clock?**
**No.** The five-year clock is tied to the *major* version's initial release. The policy language explicitly measures support "for 5 years after its **initial release**," and the Final Release column is a fixed calendar date per major version, independent of which minor you run. Minor releases are defined as bug/security/low-risk fix deliveries numbered by the trailing component (e.g., 16.14 → 16.15) — they are not new major releases and therefore do not reset the support window ([versioning page](https://www.postgresql.org/support/versioning/)). Indeed, the community recommends always running the latest minor: "We recommend that users always run the current minor release associated with their major version," and notes minor upgrades involve simply stopping the server, installing updated binaries, and restarting — no dump/reload. Upgrading from 16.14 to 16.15, for example, keeps you within the same five-year major-version window; it neither extends nor restarts it.

**Supporting schedule context:** The roadmap confirms at least one minor release per quarter on the second Thursday of February, May, August, and November, with the next scheduled minor release **November 12, 2026** ([developer/roadmap](https://www.postgresql.org/developer/roadmap/)). Thus no new minor is scheduled between the fetched-page date and the 2026‑10‑07 cutoff, so 16.15 remains the current minor at the cutoff.

## Notes and limitations
- Evidence is taken from the official community pages (fetched live). The versioning page banner showed "September 24, 2026," indicating data current essentially at the cutoff; the table's 16 row (16.15, final 2028‑11‑09) is the authoritative community statement.
- The explicit statement that minor upgrades don't restart the clock is a direct reading of the policy's "5 years after its **initial release**" wording plus its fixed per-major Final Release dates; the page does not separately spell out a sentence saying "minors do not reset support," so that conclusion is an inference from the policy's own definition of major vs. minor releases.
- Vendor extended-support programs (e.g., cloud RDS timelines extending past the community date) are deliberately excluded from this answer, per the question's scope. Any such schedule would be a separate, non-community policy.
